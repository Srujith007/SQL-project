## Sprint 1.4 — Analytical Thinking from the ER Diagram

Schema recap:
- **hotels**(hotel_id PK, hotel_name, city, star_rating, total_rooms, opened_date)
- **guests**(guest_id PK, guest_name, city, guest_type, preferred_room_type, loyalty_tier, account_since)
- **staff**(staff_id PK, staff_name, hire_date, rating, department, is_active)
- **rooms**(room_id PK, hotel_id FK→hotels, room_type, floor_number, max_occupancy, price_per_night, is_active)
- **bookings**(booking_id PK, guest_id FK→guests, hotel_id FK→hotels, booking_date, room_type_requested, booking_channel, nights_booked, total_amount)
- **stays**(stay_id PK, booking_id FK→bookings, room_id FK→rooms, staff_id FK→staff, check_in_date, check_out_date, status, nights_stayed, service_requests, stay_duration_hrs)

Relationship chain: `guests → bookings → stays`, with `hotels` feeding both `bookings` and `rooms`, and `stays` additionally pulling in `rooms` and `staff`.

**1. Guests with multiple bookings**
Tables: `guests` (guest_id, guest_name) and `bookings` (guest_id, booking_id). `bookings` links to `guests` via `guest_id`; count `booking_id`s per `guest_id` and keep counts greater than 1.

**2. Stays checked in but later no-show/cancelled**
Table: `stays` only (check_in_date, check_out_date, status). A populated `check_in_date` alongside a status of "No-show" or "Cancelled" is a logical inconsistency worth flagging — no join needed, everything is in one table.

**3. Individual vs Corporate guests**
Tables: `guests` (guest_id, guest_type) and `bookings` (guest_id, booking_id, total_amount, nights_booked). Join on `guest_id`, group by `guest_type` to compare booking counts, spend, and nights.

**4. Booking channels — nights stayed & spend**
Table: `bookings` alone (booking_channel, nights_booked, total_amount) — no join required; group by `booking_channel`.

**5. Staff handling stays vs. their ratings**
Tables: `staff` (staff_id, staff_name, rating) and `stays` (staff_id, stay_id). Join on `staff_id`, count stays per staff member, compare against `rating`.

**6. Room types vs. stay duration**
Tables: `rooms` (room_id, room_type) and `stays` (room_id, nights_stayed, stay_duration_hrs). Join on `room_id`, group by `room_type`.

**7. Booking performance across hotels**
Tables: `hotels` (hotel_id, hotel_name, city, star_rating) and `bookings` (hotel_id, booking_id, total_amount, nights_booked). Join on `hotel_id`, aggregate per hotel.

**8. Room capacity vs. service requests**
Tables: `rooms` (room_id, max_occupancy) and `stays` (room_id, service_requests). Join on `room_id`, group by `max_occupancy`.

**9. Stay outcomes across booking channels**
Tables: `bookings` (booking_id, booking_channel) and `stays` (booking_id, status). Join on `booking_id`, group by `booking_channel` and `status`.

**10. Connecting a guest to their bookings and stays**
Tables: `guests` (guest_id, guest_name), `bookings` (guest_id, booking_id), `stays` (booking_id, status, check_in_date, check_out_date). Two-step join: `guests → bookings` on `guest_id`, then `bookings → stays` on `booking_id`.

## Sprint 4 — Objective-Based Analysis

Each objective below states the analytical questions formulated (matching the "Things to Consider" prompts in the brief), backed by the corresponding SQL queries in the master SQL file, followed by the actual insight found.

### 4.1 Understand Booking Demand

Analytical questions asked: bookings and revenue per hotel; volume and spend by channel; demand by requested room type; monthly booking trend; top hotel×channel revenue combinations.

**Insight:** Bookings are spread fairly evenly across the 25 hotels (98–129 bookings each) — no single property dominates volume. Website (984 bookings) and Mobile App (875) together drive ~66% of all bookings, but Mobile App carries a higher average booking value (₹17,427) than Website (₹15,548). Standard rooms are requested most often (1,259 bookings) while Suites carry the highest average booking value (₹27,477 vs ₹10,781 for Standard) — the volume leader and the revenue-per-booking leader are different room types.

### 4.2 Understand Guest Booking Behaviour

Analytical questions asked: guests ranked by booking count; guests ranked by total spend; guest activity by hotel; Individual vs Corporate comparison; booking trend by loyalty tier over time.

**Insight:** Individual guests (233 of 350) generate more than double the bookings of Corporate guests (1,903 vs 897). Average booking value and nights booked are nearly identical between the two guest types (~₹16,000–16,500, ~3.3 nights) — Corporate demand comes from fewer, larger accounts rather than higher-value transactions.

### 4.3 Evaluate Stay Performance

Analytical questions asked: stay outcome distribution overall; outcomes by hotel; duration by status; hotels ranked by problem-stay percentage; completion-rate trend by month.

**Insight:** Only 66.2% of stays end in "Checked-out" (2,117 of 3,200). No-shows (16.9%) and Cancellations (15.0%) combined mean roughly 1 in 3 stays never completes. Problem rates vary meaningfully by hotel: the worst performer (StayPoint Kochi Palace, ~39.2%) runs well above the best performers, suggesting hotel-level operational factors rather than random variation.

### 4.4 Understand Staff and Room Performance

Analytical questions asked: stays handled per staff member vs. their rating; outcome mix by staff member; stay duration by staff member; room usage by room type; top individual rooms by stay count and service requests.

**Insight:** Workload is fairly balanced among the busiest staff (56–59 stays each), and higher caseload doesn't track with lower ratings — top-volume staff include both a 4.82-rated and a 3.15-rated employee. Suites carry the highest average price per night (₹8,865); Standard rooms see the most stays (1,440), consistent with Standard as the volume driver and Suite as the premium tier.

### 4.5 Identify Booking and Stay Problems

Analytical questions asked: which bookings ended in cancellation/no-show; overall problem-pattern distribution; service-request volume vs. outcome; hotels ranked by absolute problem-stay count; channel vs. problem-stay percentage.

**Insight:** Almost all No-shows and Cancellations carry zero service requests — these stays never reached the service-delivery stage, so the drop-off happens upstream of in-stay service quality, not because of poor service. Problem-stay rates are fairly close across channels (29.8% Walk-in to 33.8% Mobile App), so channel alone is a weak predictor — hotel-level differences (4.3) are the stronger, more actionable pattern.

## Final Conclusions and Recommendations

1. **Investigate high-problem hotels individually.** Since No-show/Cancellation rates cluster by hotel rather than by channel, a hotel-level root-cause review (confirmation practices, deposit/cancellation policy, local market conditions) is likely more productive than a channel-wide fix.
2. **Treat No-show/Cancellation as a pre-arrival problem.** Because these stays show zero service requests, solutions belong in the booking-to-check-in window — confirmation calls/reminders, deposit requirements, overbooking-buffer adjustments — not in on-property service delivery.
3. **Differentiate channel strategy by objective.** Mobile App brings in higher-value bookings, worth leaning into for revenue growth. Walk-in already has the lowest problem-stay rate — useful as a benchmark for what "good" looks like.
4. **Treat Corporate accounts as a concentration opportunity, not a volume one.** With fewer but comparably-sized bookings versus Individual guests, growing existing Corporate relationships may yield more than acquiring new Corporate accounts.
5. **Use the Suite/Standard split deliberately in room-type planning.** Standard drives occupancy volume; Suites drive revenue per booking. Capacity and pricing decisions should track both metrics rather than optimizing for one.

**Data quirk worth raising in discussion:** `stays` (3,200 rows) has more rows than `bookings` (2,800), meaning some bookings correspond to more than one stay record — worth confirming whether that's expected (e.g., a re-booking after a no-show) or a data artifact.
